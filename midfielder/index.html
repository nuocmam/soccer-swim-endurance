Midfield is not one job

Defensive and central midfielders win volume and spend most of their high-intensity running without the ball. Attacking midfielders reverse that split. Wide midfielders sprint more than the centre. Strategy (6 / 8 / 10, rest-defence, pivot versus go) is taken from FIFA and UEFA pages; drills from the FIFA Talent Coach central-midfielder series and a US Youth Soccer workshop PDF. Every number on this page appears in the fetched notes. Unverified claims live in their own section.

The argument, in four claims

Treat “midfielder” as a family of jobs, not a single GPS bin. Qatar 2022 split the centre into defensive midfielders (DM, n=68), central midfielders (CM, n=100), attacking midfielders (AM, n=21), and wide midfielders (WM, n=49). Those four do not share a physical signature, and the federation language for numbers 6, 8 and 10 maps onto that split without needing blogs.

8–19%more total distance for DM/CM than other roles (Qatar 2022)
71–83%of DM/CM high-intensity and sprint distance out of possession
55–68%of AM (with WF/CF) high-intensity and sprint distance in possession
16–92%more ≥20 km·h−1 distance for WM and WF vs CD, DM, CM, CF
  1. Midfield is not one job. DM and CM win volume. Their high-intensity distance (HID) is mostly out of possession. AM HID is mostly in possession. WM, with wide forwards, win the sprint and high-intensity metres. Bradley 2024 / Qatar 2022 and FIFA Training Centre Parts 2 and 5.
  2. Strategy language is federation language. Number 6 sits deeper, protects the defence, organises the press, and is the build-up reference. Number 8 covers both boxes. Number 10 opens the opposition defence. Rest-defence is a FIFA Olympic technical page. Pivot versus a flat midfield two is a FIFA World Cup 2026 technical page. Cover versus go is UEFA Champions League Performance Insights. Nothing in this strategy section is taken from blogs.
  3. Drills follow the FIFA Talent Coach three-phase method — Phase 1 unopposed individual actions, Phase 2 opposed small-sided game situations, Phase 3 larger numbers / game phases — plus the US Youth Soccer central-midfielder SSG progression as fetched. Grids, player counts, and durations are copied from those pages. Timed sets that the pages do not state are not invented. There is no 8×45 s midfielder HIIT on this page.
  4. Two counters, then a third. Premier League players covered less high-speed running than Championship and League 1 players (681 / 803 / 881 m). SSG 4v4 is not 11v11 for midfielders: Lacome’s CMs were the least overloaded in 6v6 and 8v8; Olthof’s match-derived relative pitch area is ~320 m² per player; Clemente & Sarmento show smaller formats inflate technical counts. Formation also rewrites the in/out HID split (4-5-1 versus 4-4-2 / 4-3-3).

How to read the numbers

Every figure below appears on a page that was WebFetched for the companion notes. Approximate bands in the papers (wide HID around 1.0–1.1 km; central HID 0.5–0.8 km; some midfielders in excess of 13 km) are kept as bands. Per-role means were not transcribed from figures and are not invented here. Youth metres are 7-a-side / grass-roots samples with different speed zones; they are not scaled to Qatar 2022. Direct quotations are sentences marked as quotes in those notes.

Sample, method, speed zones

The positional paper covering all 64 games of FIFA World Cup Qatar 2022 profiled 722 full-match outfield observations. Extra time was excluded. Tracking was TRACAB multi-camera at 25 Hz. The data provider assigned eight outfield roles. Midfield counts: 68 defensive midfielders, 100 central midfielders, 21 attacking midfielders, 49 wide midfielders. The rest of the outfield sample was 219 central defenders (CD), 174 wide defenders (WD), 48 wide forwards (WF), and 43 centre forwards (CF). Player ages are not reported on the fetched page. This is an international senior tournament, not a youth sample.

“This allowed profiling of 722 player observations in various positional roles (219 central defenders [CD], 174 wide defenders [WD], 68 defensive midfielders [DM], 100 central midfielders [CM], 21 attacking midfielders [AM], 49 wide midfielders [WM], 48 wide forwards [WF], and 43 centre forwards [CF]).” Bradley et al., PMC10765430

Speed zones on the same page:

  • Zone 1: 0.0–6.9 km · h−1
  • Zone 2: ≥7.0–14.9 km · h−1
  • Zone 3: ≥15.0–19.9 km · h−1
  • Zone 4: ≥20–24.9 km · h−1
  • Zone 5: ≥25.0 km · h−1
“High-intensity activity consisted of the aggregation of Zones 4 and 5 (≥20.0 km · h−1), whilst sprinting exclusively included Zone 5 activity (≥25.0 km · h−1).” PMC10765430

FIFA Training Centre background confirms the same tournament method: positional analyses used only players who completed the entire match; extra time excluded. Analyses used match data over a normal match or regular time plus added time (for example 90–94 minutes).

“Only players who completed the entire match were evaluated.” FIFA Training Centre, background and method
“All analyses were performed on match data collected over the duration of a normal match or regular time plus added time (e.g. 90-94 minutes), but no extra-time data was included.” FIFA Training Centre, background and method

The locomotor picture is incomplete by the authors’ own limit: the physical data omits position-specific acceleration and change-of-direction profiles, and physical-tactical integration (this HID bout equals this press) was not possible. Those omissions are listed again under Unverified rather than filled in.

Volume versus explosiveness

Total distance: CM and DM cover the most ground

“During a typical FIFA World Cup Qatar 2022 match, defensive and central midfielders covered 8-19% more total distance than other positional roles (P<0.01; Effect Size [ES]: 0.8-2.5).” PMC10765430 abstract
“Figure 1 shows that central and defensive midfielders covered the most total distance and that centre-backs covered the lowest total distance in the FIFA World Cup Qatar 2022.” FIFA Training Centre, Part 2
“Midfielders are renowned for their all-round industrious and versatile nature, and as such they covered the most ground during World Cup games as they were active during both in- and out-of-possession phases of play.” FIFA Training Centre, Part 2

Upper-end total distance on that FIFA page and in the PMC discussion: some midfielders covered in excess of 13 km. Named-player sentences on those pages are not copied here. Coefficient of variation for total distance: attacking roles AM, WF, CF had CV 10.2–12.0%; more defensive roles CD, WD, DM, CM had CV 6.7–6.9%.

“Figure 1A also demonstrates that attacking positional roles such as AM, WF and CF have the highest coefficient of variation in the total distance covered (CV: 10.2–12.0%) compared to more defensive positional roles such as CD, WD, DM and CM (CV: 6.7–6.9%).” PMC10765430

High-intensity distance and sprint: wide mid versus central mid

“The distances covered at higher intensities (≥20 and ≥25 km · h−1) were 16–92% and 36–138% higher for wide midfielders and wide forwards compared to central defenders, defensive and central midfielders as well as centre forwards (P<0.01; ES: 0.7–2.2 and ES: 0.6–1.4).” PMC10765430 abstract
“Players in wide positions, such as wide midfielders and forwards, clearly covered more distance at the higher intensities compared to players in central positions, such as centre-backs and midfielders.” FIFA Training Centre, Part 2
“For instance, wide midfielders in the FIFA World Cup Qatar 2022 offered greater defensive and attacking balance as well as width, allowing for more high-intensity running to occur along the flanks.” FIFA Training Centre, Part 2
“In contrast, centre-backs and midfielders operate in central pitch regions that are highly dense with players, which limits their ability to accelerate into space at higher speeds.” FIFA Training Centre, Part 2

PMC discussion gives average HID bands in text, not as figure-read means: both wide positional roles attained an average high-intensity distance (≥20 km · h−1) of around 1.0–1.1 km; central positional roles covered much lower high-intensity distances of between 0.5–0.8 km.

Top sprint speeds were faster during games for WD, AM, WM, WF and CF (32.1–32.3 km · h−1) compared to CD, DM and CM (30.2–30.8 km · h−1; P < 0.01; ES: 0.8–1.1). Nine out of the top ten sprint speeds at the tournament were from wide positional roles (35.3–35.7 km · h−1). Figure 1D: WF 40%, WD 30% and WM 20%, accounting for 90% of those efforts. FIFA Part 2: the top ten fastest sprints were in excess of 35 km/h and were primarily by players in wide positions.

Defensive midfielders and centre forwards had the highest coefficient of variation (CV: 30.9–35.9% and 67.7–67.8%) for distances at ≥20 and ≥25 km · h−1 compared to other positional roles. A DM can look like a volume player at Zones 1–3 or like a more explosive profile; the CV is the warning not to average them into one “midfielder HID.”

In possession versus out of possession

This is the physical fact that makes 6 / 8 / 10 more than shirt numbers. Defensive and central midfielders covered a greater proportion of their distance at higher intensities (≥20 and ≥25 km · h−1) out of possession (71–83%; P<0.01; ES: 1.4–3.0). Attacking midfielders, wide and centre forwards covered more in possession (55–68%; P<0.01; ES: 1.6–3.3).

Finer split from PMC results. Players with a vital defensive duty (CD, WD, DM, CM, WM) covered a greater proportion of overall distance out of possession than WF (48–49% vs 44%; P < 0.01; ES: 0.6–0.8). Defensive roles CD and DM cover a greater proportion of HID and sprint out of possession than AM, WF and CF (71–77% vs 40–44% at ≥20 km · h−1; 73–83% vs 31–37% at ≥25; P < 0.01; ES: 1.7–3.0 and 1.4–3.0). In contrast AM, WF and CF covered a higher proportion of HID and sprint in possession than CD and DM (55–58% vs 21–28% at ≥20; 62–68% vs 15–23% at ≥25; P < 0.01; ES: 1.8–3.3 and 1.6–3.1).

“Centre-backs and defensive midfielders cover around 70-85% of their high-intensity and sprint distances without the ball due to their specific duties (e.g. pressing, defensive recoveries and transitions).” FIFA Training Centre, Part 5
“This high-intensity distance with the ball only makes up about 20-30% of the distance covered with the ball for centre-backs and defensive midfielders.” FIFA Training Centre, Part 5
“Research demonstrates that defensive players will typically cover more of their distance without the ball at higher intensities during recoveries via defensive transitions, while forwards will cover more through defending from the front via defensive pressure.” FIFA Training Centre, Part 5

Team style can dwarf position. On the same FIFA page, without copying names: Saudi Arabia covered 70–115% more distance in higher speed zones than Argentina; performed around 40–160% more defensive recoveries and transitions and about 20–30% more direct pressure and counter-pressure actions per game. Consequently centre-backs and defensive midfielders for Saudi Arabia covered around 15–155% more high-intensity distance without the ball (>20 km/h) and performed 55–80% more direct and counter-pressure actions than their Argentinian counterparts. Same DM label, not the same HID.

Volume–explosiveness quadrants

PMC Figure 2B places each observation on total distance versus HID. The four quadrants are lower-left (LLQ), lower-right (LRQ), upper-left (ULQ), upper-right (URQ). Right means high volume; upper means high explosiveness. Association strengths differ by midfield subtype.

  • CM: moderate association total vs HID (r = 0.31); LLQ 17%, LRQ 43%, ULQ 0%, URQ 40%.
  • WM: r = 0.45; primarily upper-right (LLQ 15%, LRQ 10%, ULQ 20%, URQ 55%).
  • AM: small association (r = 0.23; P > 0.05); no specific quadrant (LLQ 19%, LRQ 24%, ULQ 24%, URQ 33%). Sample n=21.
  • DM (Figure 2A): r = 0.51; split upper- and lower-right (LLQ 9%, LRQ 60%, ULQ 0%, URQ 31%).
“Whilst CM mainly occupied the upper- and lower-right quadrants (LLQ = 17%, LRQ = 43%, ULQ = 0% and URQ = 40%), WM were primarily located in the upper-right quadrant (LLQ = 15%, LRQ = 10%, ULQ = 20% and URQ = 55%).” PMC10765430
“However, a small association was observed between an AM total and high-intensity game distances (r = 0.23; P > 0.05), and it was clear that they did not occupy any specific quadrant (LLQ = 19%, LRQ = 24%, ULQ = 24% and URQ = 33%).” PMC10765430
“DM were primarily split between the upper- and lower-right quadrants (LLQ = 9%, LRQ = 60%, ULQ = 0% and URQ = 31%).” PMC10765430

FIFA Training Centre Part 2, nameless sentences: defensive midfielders cover considerable total distance but HID can vary (high volume, varied explosiveness). Central midfielders mainly occupy the upper- and lower-right quadrants. Wide midfielders mainly occupy the upper-right quadrant, exhibiting both volume and explosive characteristics, although large individual variation exists. Attacking midfielders do not occupy any specific quadrant and are the most variable position from a physical perspective.

“Interestingly, central midfielders exhibit dual tendences as they occupied both the lower- and upper-right quadrants (40–43%), clearly highlighting their volume characteristics to support teammates in-possession but to also to press, cover and track back when out-of-possession [9, 11].” PMC10765430 discussion
“During intensified periods, some central midfielders exhibit box-to-box qualities and produce significant amounts of intensity to run with the ball, move to receive, support, attack the space in behind and to break into the box [29].” PMC10765430 discussion
“This is unlike more volume based defensive midfielders that were mainly found in the lower-right quadrant (60%), and were very active but mainly at low to moderate speeds (e.g., Zones 1–3).” PMC10765430 discussion

“Box-to-box” here is quadrant language plus a description of intensified periods. Qatar 2022 does not publish a “box-to-box metre” total. That invented GPS metric is listed under Unverified.

Second-half drop — more for the 10 than for the 6

All positional roles showed a second-half reduction in total distance versus the first half (P<0.01; ES: 0.8–1.3). Declines at higher intensities (≥20 and ≥25 km · h−1) were more evident in attacking midfielders, wide defenders and midfielders than for other roles (P<0.01; ES: 0.3–0.7). Because FIFA added time lengthened second halves, the paper reports per-minute figures.

“Figure 3B demonstrates that a decline between halves for high-intensity distance (≥20 km · h−1) was evident for WD, DM, CM, AM, WM and CF (P < 0.01; ES: 0.3–0.7) but not for CD and WF (P > 0.05; ES: 0.1–0.2).” PMC10765430
“Figure 3C indicates that the decline between halves for sprinting distance (≥25 km · h−1) was evident for WD, AM and WM (P < 0.05; ES: 0.3–0.4) but not for CD, DM, CM, WF and CF (P > 0.05; ES: 0.0–0.2).” PMC10765430

Relative deficits in the discussion: collapsing all roles, around 7% less relative total distance in the second half. More pronounced for defensive midfielders (9.8%), least for wide defenders (6.0%). Relative HID and sprint declined by 8–10%. Attacking midfielders demonstrated the most pronounced second-half reduction at higher intensities (17.5–20.0%).

Why teams run — HID is urgency

FIFA Training Centre’s tactical-lens page is team-level, not position-split, but it is the “why” that midfielders live inside.

“High-intensity runs are strongly related to phases of play that have a real urgency directly attached to their outcome (e.g. defensive recoveries, final-third movements).” FIFA Training Centre, why teams run
“Moderate speed runs are more strongly associated with metrics linked to attacking and defensive organisation (e.g. build-up play, various press and block events).” FIFA Training Centre, why teams run
“Out of possession, this included high-intensity efforts to defensively recover, transition or counter-press. Similarly, in possession, this included high-intensity efforts to progress up the pitch and into the final third to be an attacking threat.” FIFA Training Centre, why teams run

Roles 6 / 8 / 10 — FIFA and UEFA pages only

Not blog folklore. Sources: FIFA Talent Coach position-specific training (on-page date 06 May 2026), FIFA individual-actions / game-situations / game-phases pages, FIFA Olympic rest-defence, FIFA World Cup 2026 technical clip on a pivot tweak, UEFA Champions League Performance Insights (27 Feb 2026) and the UEFA young-midfielders piece.

“Among all positions, the midfield role is arguably the most complex. Midfielders must defend space, support build-up, control the tempo of the game, connect units and contribute in the final third.” FIFA Talent Coach, developing players using position-specific training
6

Holding / pivot

A Number 6 will sit deeper than a Number 8, looking to control the flow of the game and make sure the defence is protected. (FIFA; named exemplars omitted.)

Physical signature at Qatar 2022: high total distance; HID mostly OOP (71–83% band for DM/CM); mainly lower-right quadrant (60% of DM) = volume at Zones 1–3.

8

Box-to-box CM

A “box-to-box” midfielder who is expected to cover a lot of ground, and help out in both defence and attack.

Physical signature: dual lower- and upper-right quadrants (40–43%). Some produce box-to-box intensity — run with the ball, receive, attack space, break into the box.

10

Playmaker / AM

An archetypal playmaker whose main job is to open up the opposition defence.

Physical signature: most variable quadrant; HID more in-possession (55–68% band with WF/CF). Second-half HID drop 17.5–20.0%. Small AM sample (n=21).

WM

Wide mid

FIFA Qatar: defensive and attacking balance plus width.

Physical signature: highest HID among midfield types; URQ 55%; top-end sprints (WM 20% of the top ten; 90% of top ten from WF+WD+WM).

Number 6 — structure, rest-defence, press, progressions

“The Number 6 plays a central role in maintaining the team’s structure and controlling the rhythm of the game. They often act as the link between defence and midfield and are key to implementing the coach’s strategy on the pitch.” FIFA Talent Coach framework page
“Out of possession, the holding midfielder is responsible for protecting central spaces, especially in transition, screening passing lanes, organising the press and contributing to the team’s rest defence. This requires physical capacity, controlled aggression and tactical discipline.” FIFA Talent Coach framework page

In-possession build-up duties listed on the same page:

  • Find space to receive, including dropping between or alongside defenders
  • Scan the environment before receiving the ball
  • Adopt an open body position to play forward when possible
  • When playing forward is not possible, protect the ball and play safely to keep possession
  • Select and execute the appropriate pass to progress play and break lines
  • Control the tempo of the attack
“During build-up, the Number 6 is often the reference point through which play progresses. Whether operating alone or alongside another midfielder, they provide the main connection between the defensive line and more advanced players.” FIFA Talent Coach framework page
“As the central link within the team, the modern “6” must be highly efficient in build-up play. This role requires key skills such as receiving between the lines, connecting play effectively, breaking lines, managing space, and maintaining team structure.” FIFA individual-actions page
“In today’s game, the 6 must support the build-up, connect play to progression, and launch attacks through smart positioning, clear passing lines, space-creating movement, and decisive ball-carrying.” FIFA game-situations page

Coaching points on that page (exact):

“Constantly create clear passing lanes in the build-up so the midfielder is always a viable option to help break the first line of pressure.”
“Find and hold the right spaces to receive (in front of, between, or beyond lines) to create solutions that help the team progress.”
“Commit and isolate opponents with purposeful carries from central areas—drive at defenders to draw pressure, disrupt their decisions, and create multiple off-ball options around you.”

Three named progression patterns:

“(1/3) Vertical pass into midfield: Play into a midfielder positioned behind the opponent’s first line to break pressure through the centre.”
“(2/3) Midfielder moving wide: Midfielders can drift wide as a progression option, anticipating the next action so they arrive early and can be found by the centre-back at the right moment.”
“(3/3) Diagonal split pass: A midfielder positions on a diagonal passing lane behind the opponent’s attackers to receive under pressure and play forward.”

Game-phases page (closer to 11v11):

“Today’s holding midfielder must contribute in all phases of play, from build-up and ball progression to transitions, while breaking opposition lines with quick switches or incisive passes.”
“Work closely with fellow midfielders to recognise when to push forward to create space, or drop in to receive when space has been created.”
“Recognise when to create overloads and use numerical superiority in the centre of the pitch to play through pressure and progress the ball into attack.”

Rest-defence

“In the modern game, the defensive structure a team adopts when in control of the ball – commonly known as a rest defence – has emerged as a vital step to achieving defensive stability and establishing a platform on which to apply an effective counter-press.” FIFA Training Centre, Olympic football tournament technical page

Spain example on that page (family names omitted): with one full-back pushing up, the other full-back came inside to form a box rest-defence alongside a central midfielder wearing 6 and the centre-back pairing. When possession was lost, that rest-defence prepared the team to deal with counters and provided a platform to counter-press.

Rest-defence spacing numbers that appear in blogs (for example a 5–8 m DM–CB gap, or 8–12 m between lines) were not on the fetched UEFA/FIFA pages. They are listed under Unverified and are not used as prescriptions.

Pivot versus a flat midfield two

FIFA Training Centre technical clip on Brazil (World Cup 2026): a 1-4-4-2 midfield two was overloaded centrally against a 1-4-2-3-1; the switch was to 1-4-3-3 with a single defensive-midfield pivot. Family names on that page are not copied.

In a flat midfield two, when either centre-midfielder came out to press, the space between Brazil’s midfield and defensive line became vulnerable, especially against an opponent 1-4-2-3-1 with an attacking midfielder occupying that space. The wide midfielder needed to come in narrow, but a flat line still left passing lanes into the space in front of the centre-backs.

In 1-4-3-3 the pivot held and marshalled central space; out of possession could orchestrate the press and anticipate interceptions; in possession supported build-up so forwards and attacking midfielders could create options ahead of the ball. Two 8s covered territory left and right of the pivot.

“His role was also crucial in transitions where he provided safety. In transitions to defend, he was an extra layer of protection. In transitions to attack, he could release forward players early and give them the freedom to commit to the attack, knowing he was taking care of the space behind them.” FIFA World Cup 2026 technical page (job language; name omitted)
“Operating in the pivot role allows him to cover space for committed defenders and protect central space.”
“Once he wins the ball, his focus switches to supporting the build-up, covering space for advancing defenders on the ball, and providing passing options to get out of pressure.”

Cover versus go — UEFA

“The lexicon of football has evolved to the point that references to the work of a number six, eight or ten now roll off the tongue of even the most casual of fans. But what exactly does a six or eight or ten do? And given the fluidity of today's tactical schemes, can a player fill more than one of those roles at the same time?” UEFA Champions League Performance Insights, 27 Feb 2026
“In the process, we will see how each player offers his own examples of four key functions – running, defending, passing and attacking – one as an attacking midfielder and the other as a deeper midfielder.” Same UEFA page

On the No10 (roving): constant movement to link play and run in behind; pressing work when out of possession is given as a reason that player covered more ground than any other in that game. A figure of 12.36 km is on the page for that single-match No10 observation. It is not a role mean and is not treated as a position benchmark (see Unverified).

“Another aspect of his performance was his pressing work when Benfica were out of possession – another reason why he covered more ground than any other player in the game.” UEFA CL Performance Insights

On the No6: protect the back four; stop counters; drop into the box to defend crosses; constant scanning; win duels; recover the ball; use it in a straightforward and efficient fashion.

“Amid the categorising of midfield and attacking players in set roles – as a six or eight or ten – there is still a need for players to break forward from the middle of the pitch and get into the final third to support attacks.” UEFA CL Performance Insights

UEFA technical observer on that page: if the No6 advances, another team-mate must cover the space vacated. Cover-versus-go understanding between two central midfielders is described as vital: one sits, the other goes; “You get in the box, I stay” or the reverse. Observer and historic player names are not copied.

“A key lesson here is to gain an understanding of which midfielder should move forward and which should provide cover and balance.” UEFA young-midfielders piece
“Decision-making of CM: to move forward or provide defensive balance and support beneath the ball?” Same UEFA page

Session rule on that page: from the recovering team, up to five players can attack (1–2 midfielders going up to create superiority with one covering).

Bangsbo GSSI SSE #125 — midfield mentions

Fetched HTML and PDF. Published June 2014. Review, not new primary data. Typical outfield distance (not position-split): 10–13 km. Aerobic tax (not position-split): average and peak HR around 85% and 98% of maximal; average VO2 around 70% of maximum; 150–250 brief intense actions.

“The activity profile and demands on a player are determined by his/her positional role in the team.”
“Midfielders covered the longest distances. However, there are marked differences between players within the same position (Figure 1), which may be related to playing style and may explain why other studies found different results.”
“This may also explain that in the study by Dellal et al. (2011), central defenders and central defensive midfield players covered the least high-speed running and sprinting distances, whereas forwards covered the longest high-speed running distances.”
“Central defensive midfield players covered a greater distance than central attacking midfielders, especially in the English FA Premier League (Dellal et al., 2011).”
“Other studies have shown that external midfield players cover the most high-intensity running distance (Carling et al., 2008).”
“In addition, central attacking midfielders covered the most distance in high-speed running when their team was in possession of the ball, whereas this was the case for external midfield players in the English FA Premier League (Bradley et al., 2013b).”
“Bloomfield et al. (2007) analysed leading and explosive sprints for different playing positions in the English Premier league and demonstrated that defenders and central midfielders performed fewer leading sprints compared to other positions.”
“It appears that high-speed running in the second half is affected by the activities of the first half, and full-backs, central and external midfield players in the Premier League, having the most physically demanding playing positions, were shown to have a reduction in match performance in the second half (Bradley et al., 2013b).”
“The typical distance covered by a top-level outfield male player during a match is 10–13 km (Bangsbo et al., 1991; Mohr et al., 2003; Krustrup et al., 2005; Bangsbo et al., 2006; Mascio & Bradley, 2013).”

SSE #125 also carries the two counters used later (Premier League versus Championship/League 1 HSR; formation 4-4-2 / 4-3-3 / 4-5-1). Yo-Yo IR1/IR2 midfield means from Figure 2 are not transcribed as numbers on the fetched pages (qualitative only: central defenders lower Yo-Yo IR1; no IR2 positional difference) and are not invented here.

Youth versus elite — paper samples, not a scaled job

U10 7-a-side tournament (Sensors 2020)

Sample: U10 amateur team; age = 10.2 ± 0.6 years; height 136.5 ± 7.4 cm; body mass 33.2 ± 6.13 kg; 8 outfield players, 48 observations, 6 consecutive matches same day; pitch 40 × 62 m; 7-a-side; unlimited substitutions; ≥10 min/game; GPS 15 Hz. Positions collapsed to FW / MF / DF (not 6/8/10).

“Differences between playing positions were found in the total distance covered by the midfielders. They covered higher total distances than the defenders (+1167 m; 95% CI: 411 to 1922 m; effect size (ES) = 1.41; p < 0.05) and forwards (+1388 m; CI 95%: 712 a 2063 m; TE = 0.85; p < 0.05).” PMC7729596
“Midfielders covered more distance in high-intensity zones (Zone 5 and Zone 6) and performed more high-intensity actions (VMaxACC, MaxACC, TDACC and HI accelerations) than defenders and forwards.”
“The present study indicates that the physical performance of U10 football players in a tournament with different matches on the same day is influenced by the playing position and is dependent on the level difference between opponents.”

Counter A — more HSR is not a higher league

On SSE #125 (fetched HTML and PDF), citing Bradley et al. 2013a: players in the Championship and League 1 performed more high-speed running (>19 km/h) than those in the Premier League (803, 881 and 681 m respectively), which was also the case for sprinting (308, 360 and 248 m). This accounted for all positions. Sample sizes on the SURE/repository abstract: Premier League n=190, Championship n=155, League 1 n=366 match observations; Yo-Yo subsample PL 56, Championship 61, League 1 32.

“Along the same lines, a study comparing the match performance of players in the top three competitive standards of English football found that players in the second (Championship) and third (League 1) categories performed more high-speed running (>19 km/h) than those in the Premier League (803, 881 and 681 m, respectively), which was also the case for sprinting (308, 360 and 248 m, respectively) (Bradley et al., 2013a). This accounted for all positions.” SSE #125
“In addition, a group of players (n=20) changing teams and moving down from the Premier League to the Championship League covered more distance with high-intensity running (1103 vs. 995 m), whereas no difference was observed for players moving up from Championship to Premier (945 vs. 1021 m).”
“The differences may be related to playing style, with Premier League teams utilising possession tactics rather than the long ball tactic typically used at lower standards, demonstrating the major influence of tactics on physical performance.”
“It is interesting that the high-speed distance covered was markedly greater for the lower level team when the ball was out of play.”
“No difference in the Yo-Yo Intermittent Endurance Test Level 2 was observed between the various groups, suggesting that the differences were not due to differences in physical capacity.”

Implication for midfielders: more HID/HSR is not automatically “higher level.” Possession style can suppress HSR even in midfield. SSE #125 also notes successful Italian teams covered 4–12% less HID than unsuccessful teams but more distance in possession (Rampinini et al., 2009) — same page.

Counter B — formation rewrites the job

SSE #125, citing the 2011 English FA Premier League formation study (formation numbers below are on the fetched GSSI PDF; game/player counts for that 2011 paper were not confirmed on a successfully fetched full text):

“No differences were observed in total distance covered or high-intensity running between the 4-4-2, 4-3-3 and 4-5-1 formations, but players in a 4-5-1 formation performed less very-high-intensity running when their team was in possession and more when their team was not in possession compared to the 4-4-2 and 4-3-3 formations.”
“Not much difference, however, was observed in the individual positions, except that attackers in a 4-3-3 performed about 30% more high-intensity running than attackers in the 4-4-2 and 4-5-1 formations.”
“Generally, the results suggest that playing formation does not influence the overall activity profiles of players, except for attackers, but does have an impact on very high-intensity running activity, with and without ball possession, and some technical elements of performance.”

Do not invent a midfield-metre delta for 4-4-2 versus 4-3-3 versus 4-5-1; SSE #125 says “not much difference” for individual positions except attackers. FIFA World Cup 2026 technical page is a live illustration: same holding player, different job in a flat 4-4-2 midfield two versus a 4-3-3 single pivot. Qatar 2022 Part 5: same DM role, HID without the ball differed 15–155% between two national teams of different pressing/recovery volume.

What transfers — unopposed cones versus game-like pressure versus HID

Passing, scanning, receiving under pressure

FIFA three-phase page: unopposed individual actions maximise repetition and technical quality but lack real decision-making/pressure; game situations add perception and decisions in tighter spaces; game phases expose full complexity (more opponents, teammates, transitions) with lower scenario repetition.

Clemente & Sarmento (2020): SSGs keep game dynamics so players “repeat without repeating,” unlike analytical skill drills that often repeat mechanical actions; smaller formats raise pass/receive/involvement frequencies.

Ade et al. 2016: match HI efforts for CM/WM are tied to location, possession status, and tactical actions (not cone patterns alone); recommends combination drills with game- and position-specific ball work for ecological validity, while acknowledging rehabilitative/isolated drills may start more controlled.

Pressing / out of possession

Ade et al. 2016 quantifies OP tactical actions around HI efforts (closing down, tracking runners, covering, recovery runs) by position — information for position-specific drills rather than generic cones. FIFA game-phases / game-situations pages embed OOP structures (zonal defenders, 2-2-2 OOP, press after loss) in midfielder build-up practices — not isolated pressing cones.

High-intensity distance / speed endurance

  • Hill-Haas et al. 2011 (abstract): fewer-player SSGs can exceed match intensity and resemble HI interval running; fitness outcomes can be similar to generic training.
  • Ade et al. 2014: speed-endurance running drives higher HR/lactate/RPE and HID; SSGs drive higher acceleration/deceleration load — different stimulus for “HID-like” match actions.
  • Fransson et al. 2018: added SET running improved Yo-Yo IR2 and muscle CS more than added 6v6 SSG; SSG covered more total distance but far less HIR/HSR in the intervention bouts — SSG alone may under-dose pure high-speed running relative to SET (~4× HIR and ~16× HSR in SET).
  • Olthof et al. 2018: on 320 m² RPA 4v4+GK, HID and sprints rise versus traditional 120 m² 4v4+GK — pitch/RPA manipulation changes whether SSG samples match-like high-speed work.

Practical synthesis from fetched sources only

Isolated unopposed circuits (FIFA Phase 1 / half-turn circuit without live pressure) build technical volume. Opposed SSGs/rondos raise decision-dense passing and pressing interactions. Match-derived larger RPAs and/or larger formats better sample spacing/HID patterns of 11v11. SET running may still be needed when the target is high-speed intermittent capacity beyond what moderate-intensity SSG provides (Fransson 2018). FIFA already stages that progression: unopposed → opposed SSG → larger numbers.
